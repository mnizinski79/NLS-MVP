Protocol

Prototype scope: The prototype supported Manhattan-area IHG hotels only. Users who searched for other NYC neighbourhoods (Brooklyn, Financial District) received no results — a known prototype constraint. Feedback related to geography or result count stemming from this limitation was discounted in the analysis. All findings below reflect genuine design signals.

Research Synthesis

Key Findings

Four dominant themes emerged across sessions — grouped by pattern, not participant.

🤷

Value proposition not evident

The "AI Search Beta" label communicated nothing. Multiple users bypassed it entirely or dismissed it before engaging — not because they disliked AI, but because they couldn't see why to use it over the standard form.

"Putting in Manhattan, New York and my dates takes two seconds — the AI is doing the same thing. I just don't see the value." — Participant 3
💬

Conversation felt natural once inside

Users who engaged with the chat found it intuitive. The tone felt collaborative, the map pairing was praised, and the transition to hotel cards was smooth. Confusion was low inside the experience — skepticism was high before entering.

"The AI made you feel like it was working with you — offering suggestions, filling in any gaps." — Participant 6
🗺️

Map + chat pairing was a highlight

Multiple users independently called out the side-by-side map as the standout interaction — it provided spatial context without breaking the conversational flow. This pairing is genuinely novel vs. standard filter search.

"Having the map display alongside the AI panel — that I like most." — Participant 1
🚪

Booking hand-off felt jarring

Opening a new tab after a smooth AI conversation broke the sense of continuity. Users who had invested in the conversational flow expected to stay in it through to booking.

"It's a little bit of a letdown to just get kicked out to a hotel website — if you want to refine, you expect everything within the AI." — Participant 1
🎯

Hotel cards felt generic, not curated

Users wanted to understand why a hotel was recommended for their specific request. Star ratings and review counts were expected but missing. When nothing perfectly matched criteria, nothing was shown — leaving users with no fallback.

"I'd like to see options that don't meet all my criteria but come close — maybe amber and then stating what it doesn't have." — Participant 2
🧠

AI didn't ask enough questions

Users with vague, open-ended queries expected the AI to dig deeper — asking about occasion, priorities, and family needs. Instead it surfaced results immediately, feeling more like a filter than a concierge.

"I gave it a very generic prompt and hoped it would dig deeper with follow-up questions. It didn't." — Participant 7
🔍

Users couldn't tell what the AI remembered

As conversations progressed across multiple turns, users lost confidence in what criteria the AI was still applying. There was no persistent visual signal showing active filters — so users re-stated preferences or weren't sure if previous context was still in play.

"It seemed to have entered, but I didn't think I clicked to — I'm not sure how that one got in so quickly." — Participant 9
Priority Finding

The Core Problem: Value Clarity

Before any usability issue, users needed a reason to try the AI feature at all. Without it, every downstream improvement is invisible.

Users who bypassed AI search entirely

3 / 11

Would have started with the standard form even after completing the AI search task

Could not articulate the AI's advantage

5 / 11

Could not clearly state why AI search was better than entering destination + dates manually

The value proposition that emerged from the sessions:

"Describe your trip in your own words — and an AI that knows IHG's properties deeply will find the right fit for you."

Positioned as a mix of vibe-first search (occasion-driven, natural language) and concierge intelligence (IHG property knowledge, proactive recommendations). Most valuable for occasion-driven travelers and first-time NYC visitors.

Design Response

Four Changes, One Narrative

Each change is grounded in a specific research signal. Together they tell a coherent story: search by vibe → AI understands you → results feel personal → booking feels intentional.

Active search criteria — location, guest count, price range, amenities, brand, rating — appear as persistent pill chips in the chat input footer throughout the conversation. Each chip shows the active filter and a remove (✕) button. Removing a chip immediately updates results without the user needing to rephrase. This gives users continuous visibility into what the AI is carrying forward, eliminating the uncertainty of "is it still applying that?"
Progress

Already Built on the Claude Branch

Two of the four design responses have foundational implementations ready to build on.

✦ What's live in the claude branch

✓

% Match scoring on hotel cards — hotel.service.ts implements computeMatchScores() using a weighted point system across amenities (35pts), brand (20pts), price (20pts), rating (15pts), and sentiments (10pts). Scores are scaled to a 75–99 range and displayed as a badge overlay on the hotel card image. Generic searches (e.g. "hotels in NYC") correctly suppress the badge.

✓

Persistent conversation context chips — Active search criteria (location, price range, amenities, guest count, brand) are displayed as persistent, removable pill chips in the chat input footer across every conversation turn. Each chip shows the filter label and a remove (✕) button — tapping it instantly updates results without retyping. This directly addresses the user finding that people didn't know what the AI was still carrying through between messages.

Next Steps

Open Questions for Stakeholders

Three decisions to resolve before implementation begins.

Q1

What is the algorithm for the % match score?

The claude branch uses a weighted point system (amenities 35%, brand 20%, price 20%, rating 15%, sentiment 10%). Should this weighting be surfaced to users or kept internal? And should match score influence ranking order, or just labelling?

Q2

Who owns the "Why this fits" and "What's missing" copy?

These one-line callouts on hotel cards must reference the user's actual stated preferences dynamically. Should this be AI-generated at query time, or assembled from a fixed set of editorial templates mapped to criteria types?

Q3

Is in-context booking on the product roadmap?

Multiple users expressed disappointment at the hand-off to the hotel site. The proposed hand-off design is lightweight and can evolve toward full in-context booking. Understanding the roadmap timeline will determine how much to invest in the current transition vs. treating it as temporary scaffolding.
